---
title: "Conflict, Network, and Survival: How Interstate and Intrastate Conflicts as well as Network Connections Influence Elite Tenure in Authoritarian Governments"
status: working
order: 2
authors:
  - Anh-Tu Ngo
---

What makes some elites safe, while others become more vulnerable to removal from the cabinet, during international and civil conflicts? Existing research has extensively examined whether conflicts threaten or protect authoritarian leaders, while comparative research shows that elite networks and informal relationships shape political advancement, influence, and survival. Nevertheless, these two bodies of research rarely speak to each other. This study argues that conflict provides political cover for leaders (and elite factions) to weaken potential challengers, yet it does not affect the ruling coalition uniformly. Instead, its consequences depend on where individual elites are positioned within the regime's network – and on whether the conflict is fought at home or abroad. I test these expectations with an original dataset of elite networks, built with fine-tuned large language models (LLMs) and an agentic LLM system that extracts, searches for, and corroborates biographical details, covering cabinet elites in five autocracies – Cambodia, China, Russia, Uganda, and Vietnam – from 1990 to 2025 (over 6,100 elite-years). Logistic and multinomial logistic models show that domestic conflicts are associated with more cabinet exits for poorly connected elites and more reshuffles overall, while highly central elites are protected from exit. International conflicts, in contrast, reduce exits overall but erode the protection that centrality provides. Meanwhile, ties to retired elites (the "old guard") protect elites during both types of conflict, as do ties to the leader during international conflicts. By moving below the leader and coalition levels, this paper shows how conflict redistributes political vulnerability within authoritarian ruling coalitions.

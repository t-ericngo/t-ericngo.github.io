How can LLMs help with a recursive data-coding process? In many types of data collections, human coders are required to go through a multi-task and recursive process: read multiple documents (e.g., policy documents, news articles, and other reports), adjudicate between various sources when they have discrepancies, conduct further searches (or read additional documents), decide the values of the appropriate variables, and double check if necessary. The collection of data on natural disasters and government disaster responses closely reflect this process. For each disaster event, coders have to read multiple reports of the disaster's consequences (which can vary by source), determine which values to trust, and which types of information should fill which variables. For disaster responses, coders also need to identify actions of various political actors and organizations, decide which type of actors each belong to, and record their name, type, and actions in the appropriate fields of information (variables). Such a process is recursive and complex, requiring coders to conduct multiple steps over a longer period of time while increasing the risk of errors. We experiment with using agentic large language model (LLM) systems to accelerate this data collection. Specifically, we construct a system of two LLM agents: a coder and a checker. The coder reads multiple documents at once, extracts details relevant to the variables of interest (along with information about each source of information), takes note of differences or missing values across sources (if any), and enters the appropriate values to the corresponding variables. The finished product is then passed to the checker, which reads the sources and the coded data, check for discrepancies and missing details (if any) among sources, decide and execute web searches for additional documents to resolve the conflicting or missing information, and decide to update the variables (or keep them the same). We use this duo-agent system to code data on natural disasters and responses on 200 examples and compare them to (1) human-coded data and (2) human-coded data validated by experts.

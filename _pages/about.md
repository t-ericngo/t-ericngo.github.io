Thanks for stopping by! I am T. Eric Ngo – you can also call me Anh-Tu, my Vietnamese name and the name I publish under. I am a PhD candidate in Political Science at the University of Iowa. My research sits at the nexus of international relations, comparative politics, and computational social science. Substantively, I examine how political elites and domestic politics shape – and are shaped by – conflict and political violence within and between states. Methodologically, I apply quantitative and computational tools – large language models (LLMs), machine learning, text analysis, and network analysis – to pursue my substantive interests. My work has appeared in *International Interactions* and the *Journal of Information Technology & Politics*.

My research follows three connected lines. The first, which anchors my dissertation, asks how networks among current and former cabinet elites constrain or enable autocratic leaders to pursue costly choices such as purges, repression, and international conflict – and how conflict in turn reshapes those networks. The second examines the domestic sources of international conflict, from how ethnic inequality pushes leaders toward diversionary conflict to how economic conditions lead autocracies to militarize maritime claims. The third studies how great powers compete for influence through foreign aid, and how recipient states' domestic politics shape US-China foreign aid competition, including in the emerging artificial intelligence (AI) sector.

To study how elite networks shape autocracies' conflict and repression, I built an original dataset of the careers, education, family ties, and relationships of cabinet elites in authoritarian regimes from 1990 to 2024. I developed a system of three LLM agents (supervisor, searcher, and evidence analyst) that searches for, cross-checks, and compiles multilingual sources into high-quality elite biographies. I then finetuned open-source LLMs to extract relevant information in those biographies into structured, résumé-like records (JSONs). These records allow me to build networks of co-workers, educational peers, and family members among cabinet elites.

I am also carrying this computational approach into new questions. At the Disaster, Migration, and Violence (DMV) Lab, I work with Professors Sara Mitchell and Elise Pizzi, to apply LLMs to collect data on natural disasters and the responses of governments and other political actors. With My H. Nguyen, I am also adapting the LLM framework to build a database of provincial elites in Vietnam and their ties to national patrons.

While my work is global in scope, it also draws on the politics and international relations of East and Southeast Asia, particularly elite dynamics and authoritarian politics in the region. My co-authored work also examines nationalism and political participation in Vietnam's digital sphere.

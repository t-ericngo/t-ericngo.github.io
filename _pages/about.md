Thanks for stopping by! I am T. Eric Ngo – you can also call me Anh-Tu, my Vietnamese name and the name I publish under – a PhD candidate in Political Science at the University of Iowa. My research sits at the nexus of international relations, comparative politics, and computational social science. Substantively, I examine how political elites and domestic politics shape – and are shaped by – conflict and political violence within and between states. My work has appeared in *International Interactions* and the *Journal of Information Technology & Politics*.

My research follows three connected lines. The first, which anchors my dissertation, asks how networks among current and former cabinet elites constrain or enable autocratic leaders to pursue costly choices such as purges, repression, and international conflict – and how conflict in turn reshapes those networks. The second examines the domestic sources of international conflict, from how ethnic inequality pushes leaders toward diversionary conflict to how economic conditions lead autocracies to militarize maritime claims. The third studies how great powers compete for influence through foreign aid, and how recipient states' domestic politics shape US–China aid competition, including in the emerging artificial intelligence (AI) sector.

Trained in political methodology and computational social science, I combine statistical modeling with computational tools – large language models (LLMs), machine learning, text analysis, and network analysis – to pursue these questions. To study how elite networks shape autocracies' conflict and repression, I built an original dataset of the careers, education, family ties, and relationships of cabinet elites in authoritarian regimes from 1990 to 2024 – fine-grained, relational detail that existing cross-national datasets lack. I developed a system of LLM agents that searches for, cross-checks, and compiles multilingual sources into high-quality elite biographies, and then fine-tuned open-source LLMs to turn those biographies into structured, résumé-like records. These records allow me to build networks of co-workers, educational peers, and family members among cabinet elites.

I am now carrying this computational approach into new questions. At the Disaster, Migration, and Violence Lab (led by Professors Sara Mitchell and Elise Pizzi), I study how elite politics, political crises, and electoral violence shape government responses to natural disasters. With My H. Nguyen, I am also building a database of provincial elites in Vietnam and their ties to national patrons.

While my work is global in scope, it draws heavily on the politics and international relations of East and Southeast Asia, particularly elite dynamics and authoritarian politics. My co-authored work also examines nationalism and political participation in Vietnam's digital sphere.

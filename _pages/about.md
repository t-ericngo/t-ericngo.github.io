Thanks for stopping by! I am T. Eric Ngo – you can also call me Anh-Tu, my Vietnamese name and the name I publish under. I am a PhD candidate in Political Science at the University of Iowa. My research sits at the nexus of international relations, comparative politics, and computational social science. Substantively, my research revolves around three directions: (1) how political elites and domestic factors shape – and are shaped by – conflict and political violence within and between states, (2) how recipient states' domestic politics shape great powers compete for influence through foreign aid, including in the artificial intelligence (AI) sector, and (3) how the international and domestic conditions shape government responses to environmental shocks, particularly natural disasters. Methodologically, I apply quantitative and computational tools – large language models (LLMs), machine learning, text analysis, and network analysis – to pursue my substantive interests. My work has appeared in *International Interactions* and the *Journal of Information Technology & Politics*.

My dissertation investigates how elite networks make autocracies' more prone to conflict and repression - as well as how international and civil conflict can allow the leader to reshape this network. To do this, I use computational tools to build an original cross-national dataset of the careers, education, family ties, and relationships of cabinet elites in authoritarian regimes from 1990 to 2024. First, I designed a system of three LLM agents (supervisor, searcher, and evidence analyst) that searches for, cross-checks, and compiles multilingual sources into high-quality elite biographies. I then finetuned open-source LLMs to extract relevant information in those biographies into structured, résumé-like records (JSONs). These allow me to build networks of co-workers, educational peers, and family members among cabinet elites.

I am also applying these computational approaches into new questions. At the Disaster, Migration, and Violence (DMV) Lab, I work with Professors Sara Mitchell and Elise Pizzi to use LLMs to collect data on natural disasters and the responses of governments and non-governmental actors. With My H. Nguyen, I am also adapting the LLM framework to build a database of provincial elites in Vietnam and their ties to national patrons.

While my work is global in scope, it also draws from the politics and international relations of East and Southeast Asia. My co-authored work also examines nationalism and political participation in Vietnam's digital sphere.
